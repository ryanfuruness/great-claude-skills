Skill: clean-final-pass

Purpose: produce a final artifact from a chat that holds a lot of context, whether long, iterative, or covering several things at once, so that the artifact carries what was decided and nothing of how the conversation got there. It applies to any artifact for any reader: documents, messages, specs, prompts, skills.

The raw ideas, each with why it holds:

- The problem. A model's context window is its whole world, so whatever happened in the chat feels like it belongs in the output. Artifacts written from inside take the conversation's shape: a correction survives as a contrast with an alternative only the chat ever considered, an answer to a passing question becomes a nuance, the order mirrors the order of discovery, unrelated threads bleed in, and detail is weighted by the effort it took rather than by what it is worth. Editing cannot remove this, because every correction leaves a seam, and the author cannot see the seams for the same reason it cannot see the rest.

- The main move. Write the artifact fresh from a brief, by a writer that never sees the conversation, nor the author's own working notes from it, which are part of the conversation. What is not in the brief cannot leak into the draft.

- The brief. It leads with the artifact's purpose, because the purpose is what keeps a brief from becoming a list of everything. Then it sets out the raw ideas the artifact rests on, as plain conclusions without their history. It carries what the conversation actually decided; everything else, including how things happened to emerge, is open and shaped by what the reader needs. A rule that exists in the brief only because of one exchange is residue in the brief itself; state the general principle behind it, or drop it. Where the conversation reversed itself and the final position is genuinely unclear, that is the one thing worth confirming with the person, since everything downstream rests on it.

- How much reasoning the brief carries depends on the reader. A reader who only needs the point needs conclusions. A reader who must handle cases the artifact never names, such as a model following a skill or a prompt, needs the reasons behind each idea, because the reasons are what let it generalise. A brief that is too bare produces an artifact of bare conclusions; one that is too full carries everything.

- The writer also needs a standard for what a good artifact of this kind looks like, set from the situation alone, because without one a fresh writer falls back on generic shapes. The north-star skill is how that standard gets set, and it should be brought in rather than the standard being written by whoever has just read everything: a standard written after exposure anchors to the material, which is the failure north-star exists to prevent. North-star supplies the standard the writer aims at and the draft is judged against for quality, while this skill handles the brief and fidelity. The standard has to come from something that has not loaded the content, and a writer reading a brief that contains the content has loaded it, whatever the brief says about the order in which to read it. So the standard is made first and apart from the writer, from a one-line situation, and handed to the writer as part of the brief: the writer receives the standard rather than making it. How north-star is run is a matter of judgement; that the standard comes from outside the material is not.

- Fresh writers drift toward the generic, because the specifics that made the work sharp stayed behind in the conversation. Checking the draft is the step where knowledge of the conversation is the asset rather than the hazard.

- A draft can fail in two different ways. Quality: is it a good artifact of its kind? That can be judged against the standard, by someone without the conversation. Fidelity: did what mattered survive, and was only what was meant to go lost? Fidelity also cuts the other way: a fresh writer adds plausible material, such as a new claim, a new rule or a sharper principle than anyone stated, and anything the work did not decide comes out or goes to the person, rather than being kept because it seems consistent. Only someone holding the content can judge either direction. Write down what must survive and what may be lost before reading the first draft, because otherwise every difference from the old version reads as a loss and the work converges back to it, when losing things is part of the point.

- Iterate through the brief, never the artifact. Changes to what the artifact says or how it is shaped go through the brief, because a patch is exactly the seam this exists to avoid; small local fixes once the draft has settled are not patches in that sense. When a draft misses, adjust or sharpen one underlying idea and have a fresh writer start again, so every draft is a clean single pass. An idea that was in the brief and still went missing usually needs stating more sharply, with its reason, rather than a new idea beside it; whatever matters has to be in the brief, or it survives only by luck. Each round, also ask whether an idea can come out, because a brief that only grows becomes the overspecified thing it was meant to escape. Stop when a round loses nothing that matters.

- Match the effort to the artifact. A short message needs only a purpose held clearly and a fresh write; agents, written bars and iteration earn their cost on substantial artifacts. One step never scales away: before anything leaves the person's hands, someone holding the content reads the draft against what the work established, because a fresh writer fills gaps with plausible claims nobody made, sometimes ones that contradict what was decided, and only the context holder can tell. When the brief is worth the person's minute, show it to them, since it is the cheapest place for their judgement to enter; it is the one checkpoint the work needs, and the aim after it is finished work, not further questions. When this runs alongside other passes on the same artifact, match the effort of the whole combination to the artifact, not each part on its own terms. When the artifact will keep evolving, keep the brief as its source, so later changes go through the ideas rather than through patches.

- Description, to use as written: Use when a document, deck, email, spec, prompt or skill is being produced from a chat that holds a lot of context, whether long, iterative or spanning several topics, such as "write up the final spec" or "make a deck from what we covered". Not for quick messages or small edits to existing work. It decides what goes in: the content is written fresh from a brief of what was decided, so the chat's corrections, detours and order don't carry over.
