# Writing a skill that keeps the magic

Most skills are born from a session that went well. Something was produced that the person wants again, and the natural move is to write down what was produced. That is where skills go wrong. The output of a good session is the visible part; what made it good was a way of thinking, a sequence, and usually a few interventions from the person that steered the work. A skill that records the output gets a checklist of last time's findings. A skill that records the thinking gets the result again on a problem it has never seen. This skill is about the second kind, and about the writing itself; `skill-creator` covers structure, packaging and evaluation. Throughout, the reader of the skill is Claude, or a model much like it, in a fresh session with none of the context that produced it.

## Encode what produced the result, not the result

For each thing the session got right, trace it back to the move that generated it. A finding that a document used undefined terms came from reading its opening as a stranger and noticing what had to be taken on trust; the move is the cold read, and the finding is one thing it once surfaced. A sharp observation about a plan came from noticing one inconsistency and sweeping for others of the same kind; the move is generalising an anomaly to a class. Write the move. If the finding is worth keeping at all, keep it as an illustration of what the move can find, clearly marked as an example, so that the reader applies the move and not the example. A rule captures one instance of a pattern; a move captures the pattern, and it keeps working when the next case looks nothing like the last.

Be honest about what the conversation supplied that the skill must now supply itself. A session often works because the person pushed back at the right moment, said the first attempt was too specific, asked for problems rather than fixes, or reacted with confusion that turned out to be the most valuable data in the room. A fresh session has no one to do that. Ask what those interventions were doing and build the equivalent into the skill: a step that records a first impression before analysis can overwrite it, a bar for what counts as important, an instruction about what to hand back and in what form. When a skill produces something flatter than the session did, the missing piece is almost always one of these, and the fix is to describe the missing part of the process rather than to add rules about the output.

Where the model reliably goes wrong, describe the trap rather than the check. A check catches the one form of the mistake the author has seen; an explanation of why the mistake happens lets the reader recognise it in forms the author never saw. If a reviewer tends to mistake a slide about mechanism for a slide about definition, the useful sentence is the one that says why: by that point the reviewer already knows what the thing is and is supplying the definition themselves. Once the reader understands the mechanism of their own error, they can catch it anywhere.

## Write at the altitude of judgement

That reader is intelligent, capable of judgement, and certain to face situations the author did not anticipate. Prose that explains what matters and why lets that intelligence decide how and when to apply it; a list of rules asks it to match patterns and leaves it stranded the moment the pattern is new. So explain the reasoning behind each piece of guidance, say what it is for, and trust the reader with the application. This is not a rule against rules. Some skills exist precisely to carry hard specifics, such as an API's constraints, a file format's quirks, a brand's exact colours, a limit that must not be exceeded, and in those skills the specifics are the value and prose would only blur them. The question to ask of each sentence is whether its value lies in the exact constraint or in the understanding; write the first as a rule and the second as an explanation, and expect most skills to be mostly the second.

Calibrate against what Claude already does. An instruction is a change from default behaviour, so before writing one, ask whether Claude would already behave that way without it; the writer is the same kind of mind as the reader and can consult its own behaviour directly. Much of what a session seems to teach is something the model does by default, and writing it down adds weight without changing anything, while inviting the reader to second-guess judgement that was already sound. A sentence earns its place by describing something Claude would not otherwise think or do. Most such sentences describe a good way of working on its own terms; some name a trap, and those belong when the trap is one any reader would fall into, not a wrong turn particular to the session that produced the skill.

The standard to aim for is the way a person describes how they want to be worked with: a few paragraphs that give someone a clear sense of what matters and enough of the reasoning to handle cases the description never mentions. If a draft reads instead like a compliance document, it has slipped below the right altitude.

## Write it fresh, as one thing, for a stranger

The reader was not in the conversation that produced the skill, may encounter it on a public repository, and has no memory of what was tried first or what the person clarified along the way. Everything in it must make sense to that reader. This rules out the residue that conversations leave behind: a warning against something that was only ever a wrong turn in that one session, a nuance that was added because the person asked a follow-up question for their own understanding, a paragraph that only exists because a particular colleague raised a particular point. If the session contained a correction of the form "not this, but that", the skill says "that" and explains why, and the rejected alternative disappears unless a reader with no history would genuinely be tempted by it. The same goes for references to the session itself: nothing in a skill should say "earlier", "as discussed", or "the user wanted".

Cohesion matters as much as content. A skill that has been revised several times tends to accrete: a paragraph added for one gap, a sentence bolted on for another, until it reads like a committee's minutes. When a revision is needed, reread the whole and rewrite it as one piece in one voice, with each part in the place a first-time reader would expect to find it. The test is whether the skill reads as a single mind explaining something it understands, rather than a record of the process that produced it.

## Test it against the session that produced it

When a skill exists to capture what a particular session did well, there is a natural test of whether it has: given the skill and the same inputs, would a fresh session arrive at a result of the same quality? Run this in the mind first. Picture the reader with only the skill and the original material, walk through what it would do at each point, and notice where it would go flat, skip a step that mattered, or need a nudge the session got from the person. Each of those places is a missing piece of the process, and the fix is to describe that piece, not to add a rule about the output it should have produced. Where a fresh session or a subagent is available, the test can be run for real: give it the skill and the inputs with nothing else, and compare what comes back with what the session produced. The comparison is about the kind and depth of thinking rather than the exact findings, since a sound method will not always surface the same things twice, but the differences in what the fresh session noticed and how it ranked them point straight at the sentence that is still missing.

## Adapt this to the skill in hand

The altitude question depends on what the skill is for, and a skill whose whole point is a precise procedure should not be softened into reflections. But the discipline of writing for a stranger, removing the session's residue, and encoding the process that produced the result rather than the result itself, applies to every skill, whatever its altitude. When in doubt, imagine handing the skill to a fresh Claude session with no memory of you or of this conversation and asking it to do the task tomorrow; whatever it would need explained, and whatever would puzzle it, tells you what to change.
