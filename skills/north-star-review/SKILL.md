# Reviewing against a north star

A review that starts from the artifact is calibrated by the artifact. Whatever is in front of the reviewer sets the frame: it comments on what the piece contains and how well each part is done, and rarely notices what is missing, because an absence is not salient. It also drifts into the piece's own terms. A pitch deck that spends four slides on its technology gets a review of the technology slides, and nobody asks where the market slide went. The way out is to commit to a standard before the material can shape it, then review against that standard.

## Set the standard without the artifact

Give a fresh agent a description of the situation and nothing else: what kind of artifact this is, who it is for, and what it needs to achieve. For example, "a seed-stage pitch deck for VC investors", "a PRD an engineering team will estimate from", or "onboarding screens for first-time users of a B2B analytics tool". Keep everything from inside the artifact out: its thesis, its numbers, its structure, its wording. The line matters in both directions. Too little situation produces a generic standard that would fit any deck, and any content bends the standard toward what the artifact already does. When unsure which side a detail falls on, ask whether it would also be true of a different artifact made for the same purpose. If it would, it is situation. Take the situation from the person's request and what you know of their context, and ask them if it is unclear; reading it off the artifact risks carrying the artifact's own framing into the description.

Ask the agent what a strong example of this kind of artifact achieves, in two layers: the general principles it should honour, and the specific conventions that apply to this kind of work. Ask also what such pieces commonly leave out, since those are the gaps an unanchored review misses. Have it keep the standard short and ranked by how much each criterion matters to the artifact achieving its purpose.

This works whether or not you have already seen the artifact, because the agent writing the standard has not. Where no subagent is available, write the standard yourself before opening the artifact.

## Let the person adjust it

Show the standard to the person before reviewing. They often know what no general standard contains: what this audience always asks about, what their organisation cares about, what has already been decided. Adding that now costs a minute; discovering it after the review costs the review. Keep what they add marked as theirs, so findings can say which standard they rest on. If they are not around, or want the review straight away, go ahead and put the standard at the top of the review so they can see what it was judged against.

## Review against it

Go through the standard item by item and judge the artifact against each criterion, rather than walking the artifact page by page and reacting to what is there. A missing item is a finding on equal footing with a flawed one; say what its absence costs the reader. Then read the artifact once on its own terms for problems the standard did not anticipate, since no standard is complete, and keep those findings separate so the person can see which rest on the agreed standard and which on your own reading.

Lead the review with the findings that most affect whether the artifact achieves its purpose, each tied to the criterion it rests on, and note briefly which criteria it already meets well, since the person needs to know what to keep as much as what to change. What you know about the person and the situation, such as who wrote the piece or what the review is for, shapes how findings are framed. It does not change which criteria apply.

For a quick review of something small, skip the agent and the check-in: write a few lines of standard yourself before you look at it. The order is what matters.
