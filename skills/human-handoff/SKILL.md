# Handing AI-generated work to human readers

When an AI produces a document, deck or report at the end of a conversation that did the thinking, the content is usually right; what fails is how it reaches the reader. Two differences between an AI and a person cause almost all of it.

The first is how each reads. An AI takes in a whole artifact at once and handles text as information rather than as something heard. A person reads once, front to back, knowing only what the pages so far have told them, hearing each sentence in an inner voice and holding a few points at a time. Work written and checked the AI's way, for a reader who reads the person's way, uses terms before they are explained, refers to things the reader was never shown, and builds sentences as containers for facts.

The second is what feels important. The context an AI works in is its whole world, so everything in it seems to matter: the corrections, the detours, the details it found along the way. That is why AI-made work takes on the shape of the conversation that produced it and gives every point the same weight. A person sees the work against a wider picture of what matters. Much of this can be prevented by keeping the conversation out of the writing altogether, and most of what remains yields to explicit tests. A few calls need judgement that only the person handing the work over has, and those go to them.

The thinking is done in the conversation: what is true, what the argument is, what to recommend. This skill makes that thinking land with a person. It fixes whatever is wrong because an AI wrote it from inside the work, which can mean restructuring as well as rewording. It does not fix the thinking itself: when the work turns up a conclusion nothing supports, or a question the reader will expect answered that the conversation never settled, raise it with the person. The artifact can say a question is open where the reader would look for the answer, but it does not answer it.

The aim is one clean artifact. Fixes happen in the artifact rather than in a list of what was wrong with it, and what goes back to the person is the artifact and a few lines on anything they need to decide.

## Write from a brief

Editing an artifact that grew over a long conversation rarely removes the conversation's shape, because every correction leaves a seam. Writing it fresh from a short brief does: what is not in the brief cannot leak into the draft. This is where most of the improvement comes from. It fixes order, weight and residue at once, so the later passes only need to catch what it missed. Once the content is settled, write the brief first. If the artifact already exists and carries the conversation's shape, regenerate it from a brief rather than patching it, unless the person has edited it by hand.

The brief has two parts. The first is what the artifact must carry, stated as conclusions and never as history: who will read it, what they already know and will do with it, what they should come away with, the points it must make ranked by importance, any choices the conversation actually settled, and what is deliberately left out. An order is settled only if it was decided; an order that simply reflects when things came up is residue, and the order should follow what the reader needs first. Take the reader from the conversation, and ask if it does not say. Keep this part short. A brief written from inside the work feels the same pull as the work, and a brief that lists everything produces an artifact that carries everything.

The second part is what good looks like in form for this kind of artifact: the conventions a strong example follows, and what its readers expect to find and where. Get it from a fresh agent told only the situation, such as "a decision memo for an engineering director choosing between two vendors", and nothing from the content or the conversation, which would pull the standard toward what the artifact already does. Without this part a fresh writer falls back on generic shapes; with it, the later passes have an anchor for what this reader would miss. If it shows that something readers expect is absent from the content, that is a gap in the thinking, to raise rather than write in.

Show the brief to the person, saying whether you will rewrite the piece or edit it. It takes a minute to check and is the cheapest place for their judgement to enter. On a long piece, wait for their answer if they are around; on a short one, carry on and include the brief in your note. Then write the artifact from the brief and the source material alone: the files and data the work drew on, never the conversation, and never your own working notes from it, which are part of the conversation. For a long piece, have a fresh agent do the writing. A fresh writer fills gaps in the brief with generic defaults, so read its draft against what the work established and restore any nuance that is needed.

Size all of this by the length and complexity of the piece. Almost everything someone receives gets acted on, so that is no guide. A short piece, such as a message, a memo or a page or two, needs the first part of the brief and a direct rewrite, with no agents. Fresh agents for the standard and the writing earn their cost on long pieces built over a long conversation, such as a deck or a report with several sections. Without subagents, do their steps yourself, keeping the conversation out of view as far as you can.

## Read it as a person would, in order

Rereading misses what a first-time reader trips on, because the author holds the whole thing: a term used on one page and explained four pages later feels explained. So go through the artifact in order and keep a running list of what the reader knows so far. Seed it with what the brief says this reader brings, so you neither leave gaps for them nor explain what they already know, then add each term, name, number and claim where it is introduced. Anything used before it is on the list is a gap, however obvious it feels. Three forms are common:

- **Arriving before being introduced.** A name, concept or conclusion is used ahead of the part that sets it up, usually because the author knew it was coming.
- **References that only resolve with the full context.** Phrases like "the hardest part" or "the two options" point at something the reader was never shown. The first mention of anything significant says what it is and why it is here.
- **The working vocabulary of the making.** Labels, codes, stage numbers and shorthand coined during the work read as ordinary words to the author and as puzzles to the reader. Use plain words, or define them where they first appear.

Pause after the opening, the first page or the first screen, and ask whether the reader now knows what this is and why it is worth reading on. Many readers stop there. An opening can still build toward its point rather than state it, as long as the reader knows where they are and has a reason to continue.

Fix each gap where you find it, usually by moving an introduction earlier, adding the clause that says what something is, or reordering so that each part sets up the next. Do not skip one because it looks small: a single undefined phrase looks minor next to a structural idea, yet it is exactly where a real reader stops following. Equally, leave alone what would not change the reader's experience. The aim is a piece that reads cleanly, not one that has been inspected.

## Cut to what a person can carry

A person reading a page takes away one or two things; if the page offers ten at the same volume, they take away whichever caught their eye. An AI does not feel that limit, and because everything in its context seems to matter, its instinct for which of the ten belong is unreliable, so cutting needs explicit tests.

The first is for residue the brief did not keep out. It reads fine on an in-order pass, because every reference in it resolves, but it takes recognisable forms: a contrast with an alternative no reader would consider, there only because it was an earlier draft; a caveat that answers a question someone asked for their own understanding; points ordered by when corrections arrived; a detail given room because it took effort to establish. Cut these, or state the conclusion they were circling.

For everything else, ask whether this reader would miss it, judged against the brief and what readers of this kind of artifact expect, not against how present it was in the work. Where the answer turns on a fact you can check, such as how often something happens, check it. Where it turns on judgement only the person has, keep your default and add the question to your note.

Give each page or section one job at one altitude: an overview page should not also carry the mechanism, and a page of evidence should not also draw the conclusion. Read only the headings, in order; as claims rather than topic labels, they should tell the whole story. And make every visual encoding mean something. Equal-sized blocks imply equal importance, bold implies emphasis, a box implies a separate thing, and a colour implies a category, so when these are used for symmetry or decoration the reader decodes meaning that is not there. Repetition, legends nothing uses, numbers that show effort rather than inform, and hedges go.

When a cut would lose substance, such as the evidence a skeptical reader may want or the full table behind a summary, move it instead of deleting it: to an appendix, or a sidecar file alongside the artifact, with a one-line pointer where it was. Shrinking type or compressing sentences to make things fit is the sign that this is needed.

## Write each sentence the way you would say it

A person hears a sentence as they read it and holds two or three things while it lasts; an AI writes sentences as containers for facts and never hears them. Text written that way can be accurate throughout and still read as assembled rather than written. Four habits produce it:

- **The sources' words instead of the reader's.** An AI names things the way the documents it worked from name them: a specification's version label, a numbered item from a framework, a term of art from the research. To the reader each label is a spike to be resolved, and to an insider it shows the text was stitched together from files. Name things the way this reader names them in conversation, and keep source labels in citations or the appendix.
- **Packing.** Semicolon chains, fragments, stacked nouns, caveats slotted into the middle. Give each sentence one idea, and state a caveat once, where it applies.
- **Writing around rules.** Style rules phrased as bans remove natural phrasings without supplying replacements, so the writing routes around them and comes out stilted. When following a rule produces a sentence no one would say, find the plain way to say it, which usually needs neither the banned move nor the workaround.
- **Uniform rhythm.** Every cell in the same grammatical shape, every note opening with the same bold lead-in, lists that always come in threes. Repeated sentence patterns are the textual version of equal-sized boxes.

The test is the ear: read each sentence as if saying it across a table to this reader, and if you would not say it that way, write what you would say. A label or table cell is held to the same test. Do this as its own pass after the structure has settled, because structural fixes rewrite sentences, and a pass that is also judging structure stops hearing them.

## Look at what the reader will see

People see the rendered output, not the source. Overlaps, awkward wraps, a word stranded on its own line and drift in styles between pages exist only in the render. Look at every page as it renders, and at all of them side by side, and ask on each where the eye lands first and whether that is the point of the page. If the artifact has a rendered form you cannot see, say so in your note rather than skipping this silently.

## For long pieces, get eyes that don't share your context

On a long piece, the author cannot fully unknow what it knows, and a fresh reviewer can stand in for the reader. Give it only the rendered artifact, one line on who will read it, and any choices that were settled and are not under review. Without that last part, reviewers default to generic advice, such as moving the conclusion to the front. Do not give it the brief, sources or history, since that history is the bias being removed. A reviewer handed the whole artifact reads it all at once, like the author, and fills early gaps from later pages, so hand it the pages one at a time and ask, after each and before the next, what it took away, what it had to take on trust, and what appeared without introduction. Where the organisation's own names for things matter, ask the person how they refer to the main items and give that to the reviewer too.

Its notes are input, not a to-do list. Fix what this reader would stumble on or miss, treating confusion as the finding even when the suggested fix is wrong; put anything else worth the person's attention in your note; drop the rest. Its takeaway from each page also shows where the weight landed: a page whose takeaway is a side detail is giving that detail too much room. After a substantial restructure, run it again, since moving things creates new gaps.

## What goes back to the person

The artifact, and a short note: the defaults you took on calls that were theirs, any open questions in the thinking, and anything you could not check, such as the render. Not a list of what was fixed. If the note is longer than a few lines, most of it probably belonged in the artifact or nowhere.
