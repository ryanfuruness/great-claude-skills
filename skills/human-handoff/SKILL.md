# Handing AI-generated work to human readers

When an AI produces a document, deck or report, the content is usually right; what fails is how it reaches the reader. Two differences between an AI and a person cause almost all of it.

The first is how each reads. An AI takes in a whole artifact at once and handles text as information rather than as something heard. A person reads once, front to back, knowing only what the pages so far have told them, hearing each sentence in an inner voice and holding a few points at a time. Work written and checked the AI's way, for a reader who reads the person's way, uses terms before they are explained, refers to things the reader was never shown, and builds sentences as containers for facts. Everything needed to catch this is on the page, so a deliberate procedure can catch it.

The second is what feels important. The context an AI works in is its whole world, so everything in it seems to matter: the corrections, the detours, the details it found along the way. That is why AI-made work takes on the shape of the conversation that produced it and gives every point the same weight. A person sees the work against a wider picture of what matters. Much of this can be prevented by keeping the conversation out of the writing altogether, and most of what remains yields to explicit tests. A few calls need judgement that only the person handing the work over has, and those go to them.

The thinking is done in the conversation: what is true, what the argument is, what to recommend. This skill makes that thinking land with a person. It fixes whatever is wrong because an AI wrote it from inside the work, which can mean restructuring as well as rewording, and it raises whatever is wrong with the thinking itself instead of quietly fixing it. Several steps ask the person something. When they are not around to answer, go with the default you would have proposed and list those defaults when you hand the work back.

## Write from a brief

Editing an artifact that grew over a long conversation rarely removes the conversation's shape, because every correction leaves a seam. Writing it fresh from a short brief does: what is not in the brief cannot leak into the draft. So once the content is settled, write the brief first. If the artifact already exists and carries the conversation's shape, regenerate it from a brief rather than patching it, unless the person has edited it by hand.

The brief has two parts. The first is what the artifact must carry, stated as conclusions and never as history: who will read it, what they already know and will do with it, what they should come away with, the points it must make ranked by importance, any choices the conversation actually settled, and what is deliberately left out. An order is settled only if it was decided; an order that simply reflects when things came up is residue, and the order should follow what the reader needs first. Take the reader from the conversation, and ask if it does not say. Keep this part short. A brief written from inside the work feels the same pull as the work, and a brief that lists everything produces an artifact that carries everything.

The second part is what good looks like in form for this kind of artifact: the conventions a strong example follows, and what its readers expect to find and where. Get it from a fresh agent told only the situation, such as "a decision memo for an engineering director choosing between two vendors". Anything from the content or the conversation pulls the standard toward what the artifact already does. Without this part a fresh writer falls back on generic shapes; with it, the later passes also have an anchor for what this reader would miss. If it shows that something readers of this kind expect is absent from the content, that is a gap in the thinking: raise it with the person rather than writing it in.

Show the brief to the person. It takes a minute to check and is the cheapest place for their judgement to enter. Then have a fresh agent write the artifact from the brief and the source files alone, never the conversation. A fresh writer fills gaps in the brief with generic defaults, so read its draft against what the work established and restore any nuance that is needed. The brief remains the standard the later passes check against.

All of this suits a deck or report someone will act on. For a short document, write the first part of the brief and write directly from it. Without subagents, do their steps yourself, keeping the conversation out of view as far as you can.

## Read it as a person would, in order

The usual check, rereading, fails for a specific reason. When the author rereads, every reference resolves, because the author holds the whole thing: a term used on one page and explained four pages later feels explained. The same is true of any check that takes in the whole artifact at once. So simulate a first-time reader deliberately. Go through the artifact in order and keep an explicit running list of what the reader knows so far. Seed it with what the brief says this reader brings, so the pass neither leaves gaps for them nor explains what they already know, then add every term, name, number, label and claim at the point where it is introduced. At each use, check the list. Anything used before it is on the list is a gap, however obvious it feels.

The list catches three common forms of the same problem:

- **Arriving before being introduced.** A name, concept or conclusion is used ahead of the part that sets it up, usually because the author knew it was coming.
- **References that only resolve with the full context.** Phrases like "the hardest part", "the two options" or "the second round" point at something the author holds and the reader was never shown. The first mention of anything significant must say what it is and why it is here, at that point and not later.
- **The working vocabulary of the making.** Labels, codes, abbreviations, stage numbers, file names and shorthand coined while doing the work read as ordinary words to the author and as noise or puzzles to the reader. Replace them with plain words, or define them where they first appear.

Pause after the opening, the first page or the first screen, and ask whether the reader now knows what this is and why it is worth reading on. Many readers stop there. This is a check on orientation, so an opening can still build toward its point rather than state it, as long as the reader knows where they are and has a reason to continue.

Keep every gap the read turns up as its own item, by page, and fix each one. Do not fold them into a summary or rank them against larger ideas. A single undefined phrase on one page looks minor next to a proposal to restructure, so it is the first thing a ranked list drops, yet it is exactly where a real reader stops following, and catching it is the purpose of this pass.

The fix is rarely a footnote. Usually it means moving an introduction earlier, adding the clause that says what something is, or reordering within a section so that each part sets up the next. When the read exposes a problem in the content itself, such as a conclusion nothing supports or a section with no point, raise it with the person handing the work over instead of smoothing it over with form.

## Cut to what a person can carry

A person reading a page takes away one or two things; if the page offers ten at the same volume, they take away whichever caught their eye. An AI does not feel that limit, and because everything in its context seems to matter, its sense of which of the ten belong is unreliable. So cutting needs explicit tests rather than instinct.

Writing from a brief removes most of the conversation's residue, but check for what remains, because residue reads fine on an in-order pass: every reference in it resolves. It takes recognisable forms. A contrast with an alternative no reader would consider, there only because it was an earlier draft. A caveat or nuance that answers a question someone asked for their own understanding. Points ordered by when corrections arrived rather than by what the reader needs first. A detail given room because it took effort to establish, or a warning about an approach that was only ever tried during the work. Cut these, or state the conclusion they were circling.

For everything else, the test is whether this reader would miss it, not whether it is true or hard-won. Judge that against the brief, including what readers of this kind of artifact expect to find, rather than against how present something was in the work. Where the answer depends on a fact you can check, such as how often something happens or how many people it affects, check it. Where it depends on judgement only the person handing the work over has, ask in a sentence that names the call and the default you would take.

Give each page or section one job at one altitude. An overview page should not also carry the mechanism, and a page of evidence should not also draw the conclusion. Then read only the headings, in order: as claims rather than topic labels, they should tell the whole story at the top altitude.

An AI's default way of delivering also adds structure that people read as meaning. Equal-sized blocks imply equal importance, bold implies emphasis, a box implies a separate thing, and a colour or symbol implies a category. When these are used for symmetry or decoration, the reader decodes meaning that is not there. So every element should be something the reader would miss if it were cut, and every encoding (colour, symbol, weight, term) should mean one thing across the whole artifact. Repetition, legends nothing uses, rows of numbers that show effort rather than inform, and hedges or restated summaries all go.

## Keep the substance

Cutting to a size a person can take in can remove detail that matters: the evidence a skeptical reader may want, the full table behind a summary, the reasoning behind a judgement call. When a cut would lose substance, or when you find yourself shrinking type or compressing sentences to make things fit, move the detail instead of deleting it. Put it in an appendix at the back, or in a sidecar document such as a markdown file alongside the main artifact, and leave a one-line pointer where it was. The main artifact carries what the reader needs to follow and decide; the appendix or sidecar carries everything else for whoever wants it.

## Write each sentence the way you would say it

The same gap appears inside every sentence. A person hears a sentence as they read it and holds two or three things while it lasts; an AI writes sentences as containers for facts and never hears them. Text written that way can be accurate throughout and still read as assembled rather than written. Four habits produce it, and all four yield to the same test.

- **The sources' words instead of the reader's.** An AI names things the way the documents it worked from name them: a specification's version label, a numbered item from a framework, a term of art from the research. To the AI that precision feels rigorous; to the reader each label is a spike to be resolved, and to an insider who recognises one it shows that the text was stitched together from files. This reaches further than vocabulary coined during the work: an accurate, official label can still be in the wrong register. Name things the way this reader names them in conversation, and keep the source labels in citations, footnotes or the appendix.
- **Packing.** Fitting more into a sentence than a listener can hold: semicolon chains, fragments, stacked nouns, caveats slotted into the middle. Give each sentence one idea, and state a caveat once, where it applies, rather than inside every sentence it touches.
- **Writing around rules.** Style rules phrased as bans on words, constructions or punctuation remove natural phrasings without supplying natural replacements, so the writing routes around them and comes out stilted. When following a rule produces a sentence no one would say, the sentence is wrong; find the plain way to say it, which usually needs neither the banned move nor the workaround.
- **Uniform rhythm.** Every cell in the same grammatical shape, every note opening with the same bold lead-in, lists that always come in threes. Repeated sentence patterns are the textual version of equal-sized boxes.

The test is the ear. Read each sentence as if saying it across a table to this reader. If you would not say it that way, write what you would say. Text in a small space, such as a table cell or a label, is held to the same test: a short phrase a person would use beats a compressed sentence nobody would. Make this its own pass over every sentence, after the structure has settled, because structural fixes rewrite sentences, and a pass that is also judging structure stops hearing them.

## Look at what the reader will see

People see the rendered output, not the source. Overlaps, awkward wraps, a single word stranded on its own line, uneven spacing and inconsistencies between pages exist only in the render. Look at every page as it renders, then at all the pages side by side, where drift in styles and encodings shows. If you cannot see the rendered output, say so rather than skipping this step. On each page, ask where the eye lands first and whether that is the point of the page.

## Get eyes that don't share your context

Even with deliberate effort, the author cannot fully unknow what it knows. Fresh reviewers, as subagents where they are available, can do this, provided they are set up to read the way a person does. Give them only the rendered artifact, one line on who will read it, and which choices were settled and are not under review, such as an order the conversation decided. Do not give them the brief, the sources, drafts or how the work was made: that history is exactly the bias being removed. Naming the deliberate choices matters because, left alone, reviewers default to generic structural advice, such as moving the conclusion to the front, which can undo a choice made for good reason and crowds out the gaps they were asked to find.

- **A first-time reader.** A reviewer handed the whole artifact will read it the way an AI does, all at once, and fill early gaps from later pages; this is how a confusing first mention survives review. So hand the pages over one at a time, as separate files, and ask the reviewer to write after each page, before opening the next: what it took away, what it had to take on trust, and anything that appeared without introduction. At the end, ask where it got lost and what it would ask the author.
- **A sentence reader.** Goes through every sentence and marks any that does not sound like a person talking to this reader, with how a person would say it. Someone who knows the reader's organisation does this best, because a label that sounds ordinary to an outsider can be a spike to an insider. When the person handing the work over can spare a few minutes, ask them to skim for internal labels; otherwise ask them how their organisation refers to the main things in the piece and give that to the reviewer.
- **A visual pass.** Defects, consistency of encodings across pages, noise that could be cut, and whether each page's hierarchy points at its point.

The first-time reader also shows where the weight landed. Compare its takeaway from each page with what the brief says that page should carry: a page whose takeaway is a side detail is giving that detail too much room.

Ask reviewers to return their notes in full, with every gap and every flagged sentence listed by page, alongside any summary, and read the notes rather than the summary: a reviewer's own ranking drops small, precise problems for the same reason the author's would. Treat a reviewer's confusion as the finding, even when its suggested fix is wrong: the place a fresh reader stumbles is where a real reader will. After substantial changes, run the first-time reader again, because restructuring moves introductions around and creates new gaps.

Separate reviewers suit the same artifacts that get the full brief. For a short document, do the in-order read, the residue check and the ear test yourself, then look at the rendered result.
