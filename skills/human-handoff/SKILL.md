# Human handoff

Work an AI produces rarely fails its readers on content. By the time a piece is ready to go out, the conclusions are usually right; what loses the reader is the form. That failure is not random. It comes from the ways an AI differs from a person, so it recurs in the same shapes, and it is invisible from where the author stands, so general care and a final reread do not catch it. This skill is the dedicated pass that does: it takes a piece whose thinking is settled and makes that thinking land with the people who will read it.

## Why the author cannot see the problem

Two differences account for most of it.

The first is reading. You take in an artifact whole. A person reads it front to back, knowing only what they have been told so far, hearing each sentence as they go and holding a few points at a time. So you do not notice a term used two paragraphs before it is explained, a reference to something the reader never saw, or a sentence built as a container for four facts rather than as something a person would say. Rereading does not help, because for you every reference resolves.

The second is salience. Your context is your whole world, so everything in it feels important and everything arrives at the same volume. A person carries one or two things away from a page. Offered ten at equal weight, they keep whichever happened to catch their eye, which is rarely the one that mattered.

Everything below is a way of getting around one of these two.

## What the pass covers

The thinking is done, and so is the question of what a good piece of this kind should contain. What remains is how a person takes the piece in: its reading structure (setup before use, how much each section carries, an opening that orients, supporting material moved out of the main line), its sentences, and what the reader actually sees. That can mean real restructuring, not only polish.

Two limits. If you find a genuine gap in the thinking, something the work never concluded, do not fill it; writing it in would hand the person a conclusion they never reached. And if the piece was written fresh from a brief, a structural problem is better fixed by sharpening the brief and regenerating than by restructuring the draft, because reworking it by hand brings back the seams the fresh write removed. Local fixes are made in place either way.

When the piece also carries the shape of the conversation that produced it, clean-final-pass is the tool for washing that out. The two are expected to run together on artifacts from long chats, with this skill then making the clean result land with people. Which to use, and in what order, is yours to judge from the piece in front of you.

The conversation is where the person's judgement went, so lean on it and aim to hand back finished work rather than a round of questions. Ask only about what bears on writing for these readers, such as who they are, what the piece is for or what form it takes, and only when the conversation leaves it unclear.

When the piece is being written fresh from a brief, the ideas here can go into that brief: an opening that orients, one job per section, sentences that sound said, structure that means something. The writer then aims at them from the start, and the pass on the finished draft becomes a check on what it still gets wrong, which is far cheaper than a rework. Keep the sentence-level look at the finished draft even when the rest of the pass is light. A brief carries structure and intent well, but some patterns only show across a whole draft, such as the same contrast construction used a dozen times or every item cast in one shape.

## Reading it for the first time

You cannot decide to forget what you know, so a newcomer's read has to be constructed. You can do it yourself: go through in order, keeping explicit track of what the reader has been told so far, starting from what they already bring, and treat anything used before it was given as a gap. Or you can give it to a fresh agent, whose ignorance is real. Give that agent the piece in order, a part at a time; handed the whole thing, it reads as you do, all at once, and quietly fills early gaps from later parts without ever noticing them.

Small gaps matter, since one undefined phrase is exactly where a real reader stops. But what would not change the reader's experience is left alone. The aim is a piece that reads cleanly, not one that has been inspected, so fix things in place as you find them rather than collecting them. Give the opening the most care, because many readers decide there whether to go on.

## Cutting

Salience makes your sense of what belongs unreliable. Things feel essential because they were present in the work, because they are true, or because they took effort, and none of those is the test. Cut against this reader: would they miss it? Detail a skeptical reader may want to check moves aside rather than disappearing.

## Sentences

A person hears a sentence. You write containers for facts and never hear them, so text can be accurate in every clause and still read as assembled. Readers sense this before they can say why, and it costs the work their trust. The test is whether you would say it that way to this reader.

One common form is naming things the way the source material does rather than the way this reader would. A field name, a ticket's shorthand or a spreadsheet label feels precise to you, puzzles a reader who never saw the source, and tells an insider the text was stitched together from files. Give sentences a pass of their own, because a reader who is also judging structure stops hearing them.

## What the reader actually sees

People see the rendered output, not the source, and some defects exist only there: overlapping elements, a word stranded on its own line, styles drifting from one page to the next. Readers judge care from the look before they read a word, so look at the piece as they will see it.

Visual structure also carries meaning whether or not it was meant to. Equal-sized blocks say equal importance, bold says emphasis, a box says this is a separate thing, a colour says category. You tend to reach for these for symmetry or decoration, and the reader decodes a meaning that is not there. So give each page or section one job, make the eye land first on its point, and let each encoding mean one thing throughout.

## Using other agents

Any part of this pass where your own context is the bias, such as the first-time read, the cutting or the visual check, can go to an agent that has not seen the conversation. Use one where it helps, not by default. Such an agent sees what you cannot, but it also cannot know what the work established as essential, so what it proposes, cuts above all, is input to your judgement rather than a decision. How the parts combine and in what order is likewise yours to judge.

## Handing back

The result is the finished artifact. A note goes with it only when something genuinely needs the person, such as a gap in the thinking you left unfilled, and by default that note is nearly empty.
