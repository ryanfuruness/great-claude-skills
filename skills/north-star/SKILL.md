# North star

## Why the standard comes first

Whatever is in front of you sets the frame. A review that starts from the artifact critiques what is there: whether each section does its job, whether the argument holds, whether the prose is clean. It adopts the artifact's own idea of what it is trying to be, and it rarely notices what is missing, because an absence gives you nothing to react to. A deck with no clear ask, a plan with no account of what happens if it fails, a spec that never says who it is for: none of these announce themselves while you read what is there. Producing something straight from a pile of material fails the same way. The material dictates the shape, and the result is a tidy arrangement of whatever happened to be in the pile.

The remedy is to commit to a standard of what good looks like before the material can shape it, and then hold the work up to that standard.

## The standard comes from the situation, never the content

The standard is written from the situation alone: what kind of thing this is, who it is for, and what it has to achieve. Keep that to a single line, often less. "A two-page memo asking a leadership team to choose between two vendors." "An enterprise pitch deck to a government client." Brevity is not a convenience here; it is the mechanism. What you want back is the set of general principles an expert at this kind of work carries, because those are robust and because they are what expose a missing piece. Every detail added to the situation moves the standard a step toward the content, and a standard that has absorbed the content starts describing what the material already does, at which point it can no longer catch anything. The particulars of this case still matter, but they get applied when you judge against the standard, not built into it.

To decide whether a detail belongs, ask whether it would also be true of a different artifact made for the same purpose. If not, it is content. If so, leave it out anyway unless the standard would be wrong without it. That the client is a government body changes what a good pitch looks like, since procurement, compliance and accountability now matter; that this product has three modules does not.

Take the situation from the request and what you know of the context, not from the artifact. Its title, its stated purpose and its description of itself all carry its framing, and if that framing is wrong, a standard built on it inherits the error. A deck that calls itself a product overview may really be a pitch that has lost its ask; the situation should say what the thing is for, not what it says it is.

Nor is the situation a brief. "It must lead with cost" or "it should avoid technical detail" are decisions someone made about this artifact. They may be good decisions, but built into the standard they anchor it as firmly as content does, and the standard can then only check whether the decisions were followed, never whether they were right. Whoever invokes the standard writes the one line, and nothing more goes into it.

## A fresh mind writes it

Once you have seen the material you cannot unsee it; your sense of what a good one looks like is already bent toward what this one does. So the standard is written by a fresh agent that has never seen the artifact, which is what lets this work even after you have read it. Give that agent the situation line and the question and nothing else. An excerpt, a file path or a summary of what the artifact covers each lets the content back in. If no fresh agent is available, write the standard yourself before you look.

## Ask the way you would ask an expert

The question is what someone excellent at this kind of work holds in their head to steer it to success. First the true general principles and fundamental checks, the north stars that decide whether such a piece works at all. Then the specifics of the form: the structure, sections and storyline a strong example follows, and what such pieces commonly leave out, since omissions are exactly what an unanchored reading misses. For a pitch, the ask might be: "What are the general best practices for a pitch deck pitching an enterprise solution to a potential client? I'm after the true general principles, what an expert would have in their head to steer to success, and then the specifics of structure, sections and story."

The answer should come back short and ranked by how much each criterion matters to the purpose. A long flat list sets the criterion that decides success beside one that is a matter of polish, and the judgement that follows will weigh them alike.

## One standard, used twice

Before something is produced, the standard is the target; afterwards, the same standard is the yardstick. That is not circular. It is a spec and its test, and the test exists to catch where the target was missed. So when you are the one producing, the standard is worth setting before you start, and it is then ready to check the result.

## Judging against it

Go criterion by criterion, asking of each whether the artifact meets it, rather than walking through the artifact and reacting to what you find; walking the artifact puts you back inside its frame. Treat an absence as a finding on equal footing with a flaw. A decision memo with no decision request has a bigger problem than one with a weak paragraph, and the ranking in the standard should carry through to the ranking of findings.

Then read the artifact once on its own terms, for what no standard anticipated: an error of fact, an internal contradiction, a strength the form does not usually have. Keep those findings distinct from the ones the standard supports, so the reader of the review can tell which judgements rest on what good looks like for this kind of thing and which rest on a reading of this one.

What you know about the people involved, their experience, their constraints, how feedback will land, shapes how findings are framed. It does not change which criteria apply. A first-time author's deck still needs an ask.

## What it cannot see

Because the standard never saw the content, it cannot say whether particular content survived or was rendered faithfully; that needs someone who holds the source. It is strongest on structure and on what is expected or missing, and weak on specific phrasing, which it never sees.

## Match the effort to the stakes

For something small, a few lines of standard written before you look are enough. The fresh agent, the ranked list and the separate pass earn their cost when the artifact matters. What does not change with scale is the order: the standard comes before the material.
